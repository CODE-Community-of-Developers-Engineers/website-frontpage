An online hackathon is a time-boxed building event where participants form teams, write code and submit a project entirely over the internet, usually through a submission portal, a chat server and a few video calls. There is no venue, no badge pickup and no shared pizza table. That freedom is the appeal, and it is also the reason so many people register, open the kickoff stream and never ship anything.

<!--more-->

We run C.O.D.E Philadelphia, a volunteer-run community of developers and engineers, and our work is built around in-person weekly coding sessions and quarterly hackathons at venues like Pennovation Works. Our 490+ builders on Discord also join remote events all year, and we hear the same stories again and again: the team that fell apart on day two, the student who finally shipped a first project, the sponsor whose challenge got zero submissions.

This round-up is for everyone who touches these events. Developers and bootcamp graduates will find tips on choosing events and finishing them. Students and beginners will find advice on free events that welcome newcomers. Corporate sponsors, CSR managers and donors will find guidance on funding online events that produce outcomes worth reporting. The scale is real: the [2024 HackerRank Developer Skills Report showed 70% of developers had participated in hackathons](https://wal.sh/research/online-hackathons/), and the field has only grown since.

*Most online hackathon work happens at kitchen tables and home desks.*

> Act on this first: before you register, write down the single thing you want to leave with (a portfolio project, a teammate, a skill or a working demo). Online events reward focus. Without a goal, the open format makes it very easy to drift away by the first evening.

## 1. Pick events by submission format, not prize size

**Read the judging rules before the prize table.** Every online hackathon tells you how projects are judged, and that detail shapes your entire weekend. Some events want a live demo during a scheduled slot. Others want a two-to-three-minute recorded video, a public repository and a written description. A few judge mostly on the written pitch. Choose the format that plays to your strengths.

Prize size does affect behavior. Across Major League Hacking events, [60–70% of registered participants submitted a project, and events with $10,000+ prize pools see 75–80% submission rates](https://wal.sh/research/online-hackathons/). Bigger prizes keep people committed, but they also draw stronger and more experienced teams. If your goal is learning, a smaller event with clear judging criteria and active mentors can teach you more than a crowded global contest where you are one of thousands.

- Find the judging rubric and note which criteria carry the most weight.
- Check whether a live demo slot is required and whether its time zone works for you.
- Confirm who owns the code you write; read the intellectual property terms.
- Look for mentor office hours, which matter more online than in person.

## 2. Treat free online hackathons as a portfolio engine

**Most online hackathons cost nothing to enter, so the real cost is your time.** Free entry is the norm for remote events because organizers and sponsors cover the platform and prizes. That makes them one of the lowest-risk ways for early-career developers to produce public, dated proof of work. A finished hackathon repository with a clear README and demo video is something a hiring manager can actually look at.

The catch is that free events vary widely in quality. Some are thin marketing campaigns for an API, where the only goal is sign-ups. Others are well-run, with mentors, workshops and thoughtful judges. You can usually tell the difference from the event page: named judges, a published schedule and an active chat channel are good signs. A vague brief and a single prize for using a product are weaker ones.

- Write the README during the event, not after it, while decisions are fresh.
- Pin the project to your GitHub profile and link the demo video in the first line.
- Add one short paragraph on what you personally built, since team projects blur credit.
- Keep a simple log of every event you enter, including the ones you did not finish.

## 3. Students and beginners: choose events built for you

**Look for beginner tracks, student eligibility rules and teaching workshops.** Many online hackathons have a dedicated beginner or first-timer track judged separately from experienced teams. Student-only events often limit eligibility by enrollment, which keeps the field even. These events are where a bootcamp graduate or a second-year student can compete fairly and get useful feedback.

Youth-focused online events can reach a remarkable range of people. UNESCO's Youth Hackathon is one example: its [2024 edition set a new record with 202 teams from 68 countries submitting projects](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/winners-unescos-youth-hackathon-2024-shape-future-media-and-information-literacy). Events like that show how a remote format lets students collaborate across borders with no travel budget at all. For a first event, the best signs are a published workshop schedule, a code of conduct and organizers who say plainly that no prior experience is required.

- Search event pages for words like first-timer, beginner track or student eligibility.
- Attend the opening workshops; they often teach the exact tools the judges expect.
- Ask questions in public channels so mentors and other beginners can help.
- Set a small target, such as one working feature, and count finishing as the win.

## 4. Recruit or match your team before kickoff

**Teams formed in the first hour of an online event break up most often.** In person, you can read a room and find people who fit. Online, team matching usually happens in a busy chat channel with strangers in different time zones. Teams formed that way often lose members overnight once someone realizes the project is not what they expected.

The fix is to form your team, or at least a core pair, before the event opens. Ask friends, classmates or members of a local community. When we post remote hackathons in our Discord, members often team up with people they have already coded beside at our weekly sessions. Knowing how someone works makes the remote part far easier.

- Agree on roles before kickoff: frontend, backend, design and demo or pitch.
- Share time zones and the hours each person can actually work.
- Pick one repository, one task board and one call link on day zero.
- Decide in advance who records and submits the final video.

*Teammates who have coded together in person tend to hold together online.*

## 5. Scope for a demo video, not a product

**Judges see your video first, so build what the video needs to show.** In most online hackathons, judges review a short recorded demo, and many never run your code. That changes the goal. You need one clear story: the problem, the person who has it and the moment your project solves it on screen. Everything else is optional.

Teams lose online events by over-building. They spend thirty hours on login systems and databases and two rushed hours on a video nobody can follow. Scale has made this sharper: when Bolt ran its [2025 online hackathon, more than 130,000 people registered](https://litslink.com/blog/virtual-hackathons). With fields that size, judges move quickly, and a clear ninety-second demo beats a sprawling feature list.

- Write the demo script on the first day and build only what the script shows.
- Use sample data and hard-coded paths where real ones are not needed for the demo.
- Block the final few hours for recording, editing and uploading.
- Test the submission upload early; portals slow down near the deadline.

## 6. Build a remote rhythm with fixed check-ins

**Short scheduled calls keep a distributed team from drifting apart.** Remote teams fail in ways that are hard to notice. One person gets stuck and says nothing, another rebuilds the same feature, and by the deadline the parts do not fit. Fixed check-ins solve most of this. Three or four short calls across the event are enough for most teams.

Use each check-in to answer three plain questions: what is done, what is blocked and what is next. Keep the call short and end it. Between calls, work in a shared channel and push code often so nobody is surprised by a large merge at the end. Protect sleep, too. Tired developers make mistakes that take longer to fix than the hours they saved.

## 7. Pair online events with a local in-person group

**Online hackathons work best with a local community behind them.** The weakest part of remote events is the human part: meeting collaborators, getting unstuck quickly and staying motivated. A local group fills that gap. Some builders join an online hackathon and then work on it together in person, which combines a global field with real people sitting next to them.

This is the reason C.O.D.E Philadelphia calls itself a working community. Our 5,700+ Meetup members can code side by side every week, and many bring remote hackathon projects to those sessions to get feedback or recruit help. Our quarterly in-person hackathons, including the AI Agent Hackathon, give people a place to practice skills they then use online. For Philadelphia developers, joining a local group turns a lonely weekend of screens into a shared project.

## 8. Sponsors: fund support, not just prizes

**The money that changes outcomes goes to mentors, workshops and follow-up.** Prize pools raise submission rates, but they mostly reward people who were already likely to win. For CSR programs focused on inclusion and skills, the more effective spend is on the support around the event: mentor hours, beginner workshops, accessibility and help for participants after the event ends.

Online events can reach large audiences cheaply. [Major League Hacking reported 250,000 participants globally in 2024](https://wal.sh/research/online-hackathons/), which shows how far remote formats can stretch. Reach alone is a weak metric for a CSR report, though. Stakeholders want to know who was helped and how, so ask organizers what they will measure before you commit funds.

### What CSR teams should ask for in a sponsorship

- A clear count of first-time participants and finished submissions, not just registrations.
- A plan for mentor coverage across time zones.
- Accessibility support, including captioned workshops and recorded sessions.
- A post-event path for participants, such as a community to keep building in.
- An honest summary of what did not work, so the next event improves.

Local community partners make that last point easier. A sponsor who backs both an online event and an in-person community gives participants somewhere to go once the deadline passes, and that ongoing support is where much of the skill growth happens.
