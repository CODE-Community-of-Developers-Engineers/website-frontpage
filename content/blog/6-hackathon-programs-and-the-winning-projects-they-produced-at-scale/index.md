Hackathon project winners are the teams whose builds a panel picks as the strongest answers to an event's challenge. Behind every winner sits a program that drew enough builders, framed a sharp enough problem and gave teams enough support to ship something worth judging. That program design is what this piece looks at.

<!--more-->

We run quarterly hackathons at C.O.D.E Philadelphia, including our AI Agent Hackathon at Pennovation Works, so we spend a lot of time asking why some events produce dozens of strong finalists and others produce a handful of half-finished demos. The six examples below are the clearest answers we have found. Each one is a real program with published results, drawn from [AngelHack's collection of hackathon examples](https://angelhack.com/blog/hackathon-examples/).

We picked them because they cover different shapes: public multi-city events, a long developer pipeline, a global series, a three-month virtual build, a vetted expert competition and an internal corporate event. Sponsors and CSR managers can compare formats. Builders and early-career developers can see what kinds of programs reward finished, useful work. For each one we state the outcome exactly as the source reports it, then explain what we would borrow.

Some context on scale helps. The United States is home to [350 hackathons each year, more than any other country](https://share.america.gov/hackathons-tap-americans-innovative-spirit/), according to ShareAmerica. At the far end, NASA describes its Space Apps Challenge as [the largest annual global hackathon](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-international-space-apps-challenge-announces-2024-global-winners/), and its 2024 Global Winners were chosen from [9,996 project submissions](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/stem-engagement-at-nasa/nasa-international-space-apps-challenge-announces-2024-global-winners/). Most events are far smaller. The programs below sit in between, which makes them useful models for organizers and sponsors in 2026.

*Winning projects grow out of program design: the challenge, the support and the time teams get.*

> The pattern these six share: every program tied its challenge to a real problem or a real tool the sponsor cared about, gave builders support beyond a single weekend (mentors, tooling, a community hub or a follow-on track) and measured success by finished solutions and engaged builders, not attendance alone.

## P&G: public hackathons across seven cities

**P&G ran themed public hackathons with AngelHack in seven cities.** P&G partnered with AngelHack to run public hackathons on themes like women's health, smart home and consumer data. According to [AngelHack's write-up of the program](https://angelhack.com/blog/hackathon-examples/), the events drew over 1,000 participants across 7 cities and produced more than 230 solutions.

The detail worth noticing is the themes. Women's health, smart home and consumer data are areas where a consumer goods company has real questions, so teams were building toward problems a sponsor would actually look at after the event. That is how a winning project turns into a conversation instead of a trophy. For a sponsor, a narrow theme also makes judging easier, because judges compare projects that answer the same kind of question.

For builders, themed events like these are a good answer to the question of where hackathon ideas come from. Start from the theme, then pick one specific person who has the problem. A smart home project for a renter who cannot drill into walls is easier to scope, demo and judge than a general smart home platform.

- Pick themes the sponsor has real questions about, so finished projects have somewhere to go.
- Run the same format in several cities to reach builders who would not travel.
- Count solutions produced as the outcome, alongside participants.

## Hedera: a learn, build and incubate pipeline

**Hedera built a multi-phase program that ended with 7 incubated projects.** Hedera ran a sequence of Learn and Earn, a hackathon and an incubator to attract and educate developers. [AngelHack reports](https://angelhack.com/blog/hackathon-examples/) that the program engaged over 5,000 developers and incubated 7 top projects.

This is the example we point early-career developers and bootcamp graduates to most often. The learning phase came before the competition, so people new to the technology could get up to speed before they had to ship. The incubator came after, so winners had a path past the closing ceremony. The jump from more than 5,000 engaged developers to 7 incubated projects also shows how selective the final stage is. Most participants gained skills, and a small group got sustained support.

Prize money matters too. Hedera's 2024 #HelloFuture Hackathon offered [over $350,000 USD in prizes for winning teams](https://hedera.com/blog/these-are-the-winners-of-the-2024-hellofuture-hackathon/), according to Hedera. Prizes draw entrants. The learning and incubation phases are what turn entrants into lasting projects.

- Teach the tools before the hackathon starts.
- Run the hackathon as the middle phase, not the whole program.
- Give the strongest projects a follow-on incubator track.

## AWS: the hackglobal Global Hackathon Series

**AWS sponsored the 2019 hackglobal series across 25 cities.** AWS sponsored the 2019 hackglobal Global Hackathon Series with AngelHack across 25+ cities. Per [AngelHack's summary](https://angelhack.com/blog/hackathon-examples/), the series engaged over 2,500 participants across 25 cities and resulted in more than 350 solutions.

In 2019 this was a sponsor model built on reach: one sponsor, one format, many local events. By 2026 the same idea shows up in hybrid series where local hosts run the room and a central sponsor supplies tools, credits and a final round. The lesson for CSR managers is that a sponsor does not need to run every event. Funding a repeatable format and letting local communities host it can produce hundreds of solutions.

Local hosts are where community groups like ours fit. A city chapter knows which venue works, which mentors show up and which problems the local developer community cares about. That local knowledge is hard to buy and easy to support.

## Consensys: a three-month virtual Web3 build

**Consensys ran a three-month virtual hackathon that drew over 1,600 participants.** Consensys built its Web3 hackathon around Infura, MetaMask and Linea, using Discord as the hub. [AngelHack's account](https://angelhack.com/blog/hackathon-examples/) reports that it attracted over 1,600 participants and generated more than 70 project submissions.

Two choices stand out. The first is the length. Three months gives teams time to build something deployable, which matters for blockchain projects where integration and testing take longer than a weekend allows. The second is Discord as the center of gravity. Questions, team formation and mentor help all happened in one shared place.

The gap between participants and submissions is also honest data. Over 1,600 people took part and more than 70 projects were submitted. Long virtual events attract many curious builders, and a smaller group finishes. Organizers can plan for that with check-ins and milestone demos that keep teams moving. We run our own Discord the same way: our 490+ builders, per our community figures, use it between sessions so projects keep moving after an event ends.

## Databricks: the Generative AI World Cup

**Databricks drew 1,500+ vetted data professionals from 18 countries.** Databricks ran the Generative AI World Cup hackathon for vetted data professionals using Mosaic AI technologies. According to [AngelHack](https://angelhack.com/blog/hackathon-examples/), over 1,500 data professionals from 18 countries participated.

Vetting is the unusual move here. Most public hackathons welcome anyone. Databricks screened entrants, which raised the floor on project quality and made the event a showcase for experienced practitioners. That is a fair trade-off for a technical sponsor that wants advanced use of its platform. It is a poor fit for an event meant to welcome beginners.

For builders looking for generative AI hackathon ideas, the useful takeaway is focus. Projects tied to one platform and one clear data problem are easier to finish and easier to judge than broad AI assistants. The MIT App Inventor Foundation's 2025 Global AI Hackathon shows the same pattern from the beginner side: its three winners came from [over 1,300 participants selected by over 50 judges](https://raise.mit.edu/news/app-inventor-foundation-announces-winners-of-2025-global-ai-hackathon/) and tackled remote health rehabilitation and physical therapy. Hardware helped some teams stand out as well. [Approximately 13% of projects, including two of the winners, added hardware components](https://raise.mit.edu/news/app-inventor-foundation-announces-winners-of-2025-global-ai-hackathon/) such as IoT sensors and microcontrollers.

## UBS: the UBS Innovate internal hackathon

**UBS linked 5 cities by live video for one internal hackathon.** UBS held its UBS Innovate internal hackathon simultaneously across 5 major cities connected by live video. [AngelHack reports](https://angelhack.com/blog/hackathon-examples/) that over 500 participants across 5 destinations generated many ideas.

Internal hackathons have different goals from public ones. The winners are employees, and the outcome UBS reported is idea generation instead of shipped products. Running all five cities at the same time over live video gave staff in different offices one shared event, and that alone has value inside a large firm.

For corporate sponsors, this example is a reminder to say which outcome you want before the event starts. If the goal is culture and cross-office collaboration, ideas are a fine result. If the goal is working prototypes, the program needs build time, data access and a plan for what happens to the winners afterward.

## What they have in common

The six programs differ in size, format and audience, but the ones that produced the most finished work made similar choices. These are the lessons we apply when planning C.O.D.E Philadelphia hackathons, and the ones we would suggest to any sponsor or builder.

- A specific theme tied to a real problem produces projects with somewhere to go after judging.
- Support before the event, such as tutorials or a learning phase, lets newer developers compete.
- A shared hub like Discord keeps teams, mentors and organizers in one place.
- Participant counts and submission counts tell different stories, so report both.
- A follow-on path, such as an incubator, is what turns a winner into a lasting project.
- Local hosts make multi-city and community programs work.
- Decide whether you want ideas or prototypes before you design the event.
