[Hackathon judging](https://codephilly.com/blog/how-does-hackathon-judging-work-and-what-do-judges-reward) criteria are the named qualities a panel uses to compare projects, such as technical difficulty, usefulness, design and how well a team answers the event's theme. Criteria come before any scoring sheet. They decide what the weekend rewards, and builders shape their projects around them from the first hour.

<!--more-->

I help run hackathons at C.O.D.E Philadelphia, including our quarterly events at Pennovation Works and our AI Agent Hackathon. By the end of this guide you will have a short, written set of criteria that fits your event's purpose, matches what sponsors care about, works for in-person or online judging, and has been tested before a single team presents. The steps work whether you are an organizer, a sponsor writing a prize track, or a builder who wants to understand what judges will look for.

What you need:

- A one-sentence statement of the event's goal (learning, shipping, a social cause, a sponsor's API, or a theme like AI agents)
- The list of prize tracks and the sponsor attached to each one, if any
- A rough count of judges and teams you expect, so you can plan time per project
- The judging format: in-person demos, online submissions, or both
- A shared document where organizers, sponsors and at least one past participant can comment
- An hour with two or three volunteers to dry-run the criteria on old or sample projects

## Step 1: Tie every criterion to the event's goal

Start from the goal sentence, not from a generic list. A beginner-friendly event that exists so people learn should reward learning and effort. A sponsor API challenge should reward meaningful use of that API. A themed event like our AI Agent Hackathon should reward agents that actually take actions and handle failure, not slideshows about agents.

- Write the event goal at the top of your shared document.
- Under it, write what a great project looks like at the end of the weekend, in two or three sentences.
- Ask each sponsor the same question for their track and record their answer word for word.
- Cross out any criterion idea that does not trace back to one of those sentences.

Domain events need this step most. In [healthcare hackathons](https://codephilly.com/blog/9-healthcare-hackathon-tips-for-builders-mentors-and-sponsors), for example, a goal about patient safety should show up as a criterion about data handling and realistic use, or judges will end up rewarding the flashiest demo instead.

## Step 2: Choose four to six criteria

Four to six criteria is enough to separate projects without exhausting judges. With more than that, judges blur categories together and start scoring on overall impression. With fewer, a single strong demo can win on polish alone.

The criteria we draw from most often are below. The table shows which ones suit which kind of event, so you can pick the set that matches your goal from Step 1.

Common hackathon judging criteria and the events they suit

| Criterion | What it rewards | Best fit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technical execution | Working code and real difficulty | Experienced builders, API challenges |
| Usefulness | A real problem for real people | Social good and civic events |
| Theme fit | A direct answer to the challenge | Themed events like AI agents |
| Design and usability | A product someone could use | Mixed teams with designers |
| Creativity | An approach judges have not seen | Open-ended events |
| Learning and growth | New skills tried during the event | Beginner and student events |
| Presentation | A clear demo and honest scope | Every event, at a lower weight |

> Keep presentation as a criterion, but give it a lower weight. When it carries as much as technical work, confident speakers win over teams who built more.

## Step 3: Define each criterion in plain words

A criterion name alone is not enough. Two judges will read "creativity" differently. Write one sentence saying what the criterion means, then describe what a low, middle and high score look like for that criterion.

```text
Criterion: Theme fit
Meaning: How directly the project answers this event's challenge.
Low: The theme is mentioned but the project would exist without it.
Middle: The theme shapes one main feature.
High: The theme is the core of the project and drives the demo.
```
