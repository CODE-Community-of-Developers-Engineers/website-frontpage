A beginner hackathon is a time-boxed building event designed so that people with little or no hackathon experience can form a team, build something small, and present it by the end. Many of the people in the room are new. In a study of hackathons focused on the UN Sustainable Development Goals, the [median share of first-time participants was 72.6%](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03286). At events like those, a first-timer is in the majority.

<!--more-->

I wrote this round-up for two groups who show up at our events. The first group is builders: bootcamp graduates, early-career developers, students, designers and side-project people in Philadelphia who want to know how to get through a first weekend without freezing up. The second group is the people who make those weekends possible: CSR program managers, sponsors and recurring donors who want to know what support helps a newcomer finish a project.

At C.O.D.E Philadelphia we run quarterly hackathons at venues like Pennovation Works, including themed events such as our AI Agent Hackathon, along with weekly coding sessions. The tips below come from watching first-timers succeed and stall at those events, backed by published research where it exists.

> Act on this first: show up to a low-stakes coding session before your first hackathon. Writing code next to strangers for a few hours removes most of the nerves that cause beginners to drop out on day one, and you may leave with a teammate.

## 1. Pick an event that says it welcomes beginners, and check what that means

**A beginner label only matters if the event backs it up.** Look for concrete signals: mentors assigned to the floor, a team-matching session at kickoff, starter datasets or APIs, and judging criteria that reward learning and a working demo over polish. Free events with food and a local venue lower the barrier further for students and career changers.

Beginner-friendly design works when it is deliberate. In one community youth hackathon program, [over 40% of participants had no prior coding experience](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10159829/) in its first year, and the program was built around that fact. An online quantum computing hackathon for Spanish speakers opened with introductory workshops, and [66% of its 220 registrants rated themselves as quantum beginners](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.12119). Events designed for newcomers draw newcomers.

- Read the event page for mentor availability and whether solo sign-ups are matched to teams.
- Check if the judging rubric mentions learning, creativity or completeness.
- Prefer an in-person event near you for a first try, so help is a table away.
- Ask the organizers directly on their community channel if anything is unclear. Good organizers answer.

## 2. Rehearse in a weekly session before the weekend

**The skill most first-timers lack is coding in public, not coding.** A hackathon asks you to type, get stuck and ask for help while people you just met watch. That is uncomfortable the first time and ordinary by the third. A weekly session where members code side by side is a cheap place to get the first two out of the way.

This is why C.O.D.E Philadelphia runs weekly coding sessions alongside its quarterly hackathons. Members who have sat next to each other on a Tuesday night tend to team up easily on a hackathon Saturday. Our Discord, with 490+ builders, is where many of those pairings start before an event is even announced.

- Attend one or two sessions with a small personal project, even a tutorial you are finishing.
- Practice setting up a fresh repository and deploying something trivial, so setup does not eat your hackathon morning.
- Introduce yourself to one person and ask what they are building.

## 3. Choose a beginner project you can demo in one sentence

**If you cannot describe the demo in one sentence, cut scope until you can.** Beginners usually fail by building too much. A narrow project that runs in front of judges beats an ambitious one that crashes. Aim for one user, one problem and one screen.

A good test is to write the sentence the judges will hear: "You type a neighborhood name and it shows the three closest free Wi-Fi spots." Everything that does not serve that sentence goes on a later list. Use public APIs and open datasets so you spend your hours on the idea instead of collecting data.

### Beginner project ideas that fit a weekend

## 4. Use AI assistants openly and within the rules

**Most hackathons in 2026 allow ChatGPT and similar tools, but the rules vary, so read them.** Some events ban generated code entirely, some require you to disclose what you used, and AI-themed events like our AI Agent Hackathon expect you to build with models. Ask the organizers if the rules are silent.

Used well, an assistant is a patient mentor at 2 a.m. It can explain an error message, sketch boilerplate or suggest a library. Used badly, it produces code no one on the team understands, and that falls apart during the live demo or the judges' questions. A simple habit helps: do not commit a block of generated code until a teammate can explain what it does.

- Tell judges which parts used AI tools. Honesty usually earns credit.
- Use assistants for explanations and scaffolding, then write the core logic yourselves.
- Keep prompts and outputs in a notes file so you can describe your process.

## 5. Join a team for its mix, not its resumes

**The best beginner teams have a builder, a designer or storyteller, and someone who keeps time.** You do not need a senior engineer to finish. You need people who cover different jobs and agree on scope in the first hour.

Mixed teams are normal at well-run events. At an ASA DataFest weekend, [82 students in 20 teams from 10 different schools](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/test.12404), spanning fields from psychology to geosciences, collaborated over the weekend. Non-coders write the pitch, design the screens and test the flow, and that work often decides the judging.

- Use the kickoff team-matching session if you arrive alone.
- Agree on the one-sentence demo and a stopping time for new features before anyone codes.
- Assign one person to rehearse the final presentation from mid-afternoon on the last day.

## 6. Plan for the week after the hackathon

**Decide before demos end what happens to the project and the people.** Most hackathon work stops when the event does. A study of nearly 11,900 hackathon projects found [about 85% of changes happened within the first month, and only 7% of projects had any activity after](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544548.3581234). Repeat attendance is also rare: in one study, [only 13.10% of participants went to more than one event](https://doi.org/10.1145/3340481.3342735).

For a bootcamp graduate, the week after the hackathon is where the project turns into a portfolio piece. Clean up the README, record a two-minute walkthrough and post it. Then bring the project to a weekly session and keep going with the same teammates. Continuity is what turns a weekend into a habit.

- Push final code and a short demo video the same night.
- Write down three things the project would need next.
- Swap contact details and pick one date to meet again.

## 7. Sponsors and donors: fund the on-ramp

**The money that helps beginners most pays for mentors, food and follow-up, not bigger prizes.** A first-timer is more likely to finish because a mentor was free at the right moment than because the top prize grew. Covering meals keeps attendance free for students and career changers who cannot spend a weekend on takeout.

For CSR program managers, this kind of support is easy to report on: number of first-time participants, projects demoed and members who returned for weekly sessions. C.O.D.E Philadelphia is volunteer-run, and sponsorships and donations fund its programming for a community of 5,700+ members on Meetup. Volunteering a few hours as a hackathon mentor is another form of support, and it puts your engineers next to Philadelphia talent.

## FAQ

### Can I go to a hackathon as a beginner?

Yes. Many events are built for beginners, and first-timers often make up a large share of the room. Research on one technical event found [88.24% of participants had no experience with Web3 and blockchain technology](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2444569X24000404), the event's own focus. Pick an event that lists mentors and team matching, and bring curiosity rather than a finished skill set.

### How to start joining hackathons?

Start local. Join a community's Meetup or Discord, attend a regular coding session to meet people, and register for the next beginner-friendly hackathon they host. In Philadelphia, C.O.D.E announces its quarterly hackathons to members first, and solo registrants can find teammates before or at kickoff.
