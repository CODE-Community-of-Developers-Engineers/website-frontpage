A [hackathon judging](https://codephilly.com/blog/how-does-hackathon-judging-work-and-what-do-judges-reward) rubric is a scoring sheet that lists the criteria judges use, the weight each criterion carries and the scale for each score. A hackathon judging scorecard is the per-team version of that rubric, filled in by one judge for one project. This list collects the tools, templates and sample rubrics we think are worth your time if you organize, sponsor, judge or compete in a hackathon in 2026.

<!--more-->

We run quarterly hackathons at C.O.D.E Philadelphia, including our AI Agent Hackathon at Pennovation Works, so we have built and rebuilt rubrics more than once. I picked these resources for one reason: you can open each one and use it the same day. Some are free builders, some are published sample rubrics you can copy and adapt, and some are full platforms that handle submissions and scoring together. Sponsors and CSR managers can use the list to check how a prize gets decided. Builders and bootcamp graduates can use it to see what judges will actually score before the clock starts.

Each resource is described on the same terms. Where our own community appears, I say so plainly. The table below names every resource in the order it appears.

## HackathonHost Judging Criteria Builder

The [HackathonHost judging criteria builder](https://hackathonhost.com/tools/judging-criteria-builder) is a free tool that produces a hackathon judging rubric template you can export and hand to judges. Its default rests on a common pattern: [most hackathons judge on four things: technical execution, innovation, impact and presentation, weighted about 30/25/25/20](https://hackathonhost.com/tools/judging-criteria-builder). That split is a sensible starting point. Technical execution gets the most weight, and presentation still counts without dominating.

The companion article on the same site gives a firm rule for sizing the rubric: [pick 4–6 criteria, because more than six and judges lose focus, and fewer than four makes the score too blunt](https://hackathonhost.com/articles/hackathon-judging-criteria). Their guidance also covers sub-criteria for large categories and a short jury briefing before scoring starts.

- Generates a weighted rubric from criteria you choose
- Default four-criterion split you can edit
- Guidance on sub-criteria for broad categories like technical execution
- Advice on briefing judges so scores stay consistent

**Best for** Organizers running their first or second event who need a working rubric in under an hour, and sponsors who want to propose a track rubric that fits the main one.

## Hacktribe Hackathon Judging Rubric Blueprint

The [Hacktribe rubric blueprint](https://hacktribe.co/blog/fair-balanced-the-hackathon-judging-rubric-blueprint) is a written guide focused on balance and fairness. It agrees with the four-to-six range and explains why: [fewer than four hides meaningful differences between projects, and more than six makes judges rush and defeats the point of the rubric](https://hacktribe.co/blog/fair-balanced-the-hackathon-judging-rubric-blueprint). That reasoning is useful when a sponsor asks to add a seventh criterion for their own priority. I find it easier to say no with the cost spelled out.

Read this one after you have a draft. It works best as a review checklist: are the criteria distinct, do the weights reflect what the event says it rewards, and can a tired judge finish a scorecard in a few minutes?

**Best for** Organizers and CSR program managers who want to defend their rubric to sponsors, judges and participants, and who care about scoring that holds up when a close result gets questioned.

## CGU Ethical AI Hackathon Rubric

The [Claremont Graduate University Ethical AI Hackathon judging rubric](https://research.cgu.edu/hackathon/home/judging-rubric/) is one of the clearest public hackathon judging rubric samples available. It assigns "Socially Responsible Impact in Innovation" a weight of [20%](https://research.cgu.edu/hackathon/home/judging-rubric/) and "Ease of Use, Transparency, and Explainability" a weight of 20%, and scores each on a 1–5 scale where 1 is Poor and 5 is Excellent.

What makes it worth copying is that the criteria reflect the theme. An ethical AI event that scored only technical difficulty would reward the wrong projects. If you are planning a themed event, such as an AI agent hackathon, this rubric shows how to turn the theme into scored criteria with explicit weights.

- Public rubric with named criteria and percentage weights
- Short 1–5 scale with labeled endpoints
- Theme-specific criteria for transparency and social impact

**Best for** Organizers of AI, civic tech or social good hackathons, and sponsors funding an impact track who want a rubric example to point to.

## Vibe Coding Hackathon Rubric Example from arXiv

The research paper [Code for All: Educational Applications of the "Vibe Coding" Hackathon](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.22747) documents a hackathon used in programming education across skill levels. It used five criteria: functionality, UI/UX design, impact, prompt quality and readability, with each category weighted the same at 20 points per category. Prompt quality is a newer criterion that fits events where builders work with AI coding tools.

Equal weighting is a deliberate choice. It tells beginners that clean, readable code counts as much as a working feature. For mixed-skill events, that keeps less experienced builders in contention.

**Best for** Bootcamp instructors, early-career developers preparing for their first event, and organizers who want a rubric that rewards learning as well as output.

## MLH Hackathon Organizer Guide: Judging Plan

Major League Hacking publishes a judging plan in its Hackathon Organizer Guide that covers the logistics around a rubric. It recommends science-fair style judging, where judges move between team tables, explains shortlisting and elimination rounds, and walks through how to estimate how many judges you need and how long they will spend. MLH supports a large network of student hackathons, so the guidance reflects many events.

- Science-fair style judging setup
- Shortlisting rounds for large events
- Judge timing and headcount planning
- Notes on judging software options

**Best for** Student and campus organizers, and anyone whose rubric is ready but whose judging schedule is not.

## Devpost

[Devpost](https://devpost.com) is a widely used platform for hosting hackathon submissions. Teams post project pages with descriptions, demos and code links, and organizers can set up judging criteria so judges score submissions online. Because projects stay public afterward, it also works as a portfolio for participants.

**Best for** Virtual or hybrid hackathons with many submissions, and builders who want a lasting public record of what they shipped.

## Innovation Mode Nine-Dimension Assessment Model

The [corporate hackathon guide from Ainna](https://ainna.ai/resources/faq/corporate-hackathon-guide) describes the Innovation Mode Nine-Dimension Idea Assessment Model: importance of the problem, strategic alignment, effectiveness, feasibility, ease of implementation, ease of operation, business impact, novelty and certainty of demand. Nine dimensions is more than the four-to-six range suits a weekend event, so treat it as a source list. Pick the dimensions that match your sponsor's goals and fold them into a shorter scorecard.

**Best for** Corporate sponsors and internal innovation teams who want hackathon outcomes tied to business priorities.

- Quarterly theme-based hackathons open to all skill levels
- Volunteer roles at hackathons, including event support
- Sponsorship and donation options that fund events
- 490+ builders on Discord for feedback between events

**Best for** Philadelphia-area builders who want practice under a real rubric, and sponsors or donors who want to see how their support turns into fairly judged projects.
